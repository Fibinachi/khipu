# Khipukamayuq Colonial Testimony: A Game-Theoretic Analysis

## Purpose

Systematic compilation of every documented case where khipukamayuq (khipu readers) testified in Spanish colonial proceedings, 1530s–1600s. For each case: who asked, what was answered, who benefited, and evidence of strategic behavior. Then a game-theoretic comparison across cases.

**Method note:** This compilation is built from published secondary sources (Urton, Murra, Julien, Espinoza, Brokaw, Yeakel). I did not access the primary archival documents (AGI Seville, Biblioteca Nacional Madrid). Where secondary sources disagree or are thin, I say so. The game-theoretic analysis is my own.

---

## Case 1: Wanka Lord of Hatun Xauxa — Storehouse Accounting

**Date:** 1533 (demonstration); 1558–61 (court case)
**Location:** Mantaro Valley, central highlands
**Spanish questioner:** Pedro Cieza de León (1533, chronicler); Spanish court (1558–61)

**What was asked:** Cieza requested a demonstration of khipu reading. The later court case concerned Spanish takings from Inca storehouses.

**What was answered:** The Wanka lord read from his khipu a complete account of all provisions that had been *given to* or *stolen by* the Spaniards from the Mantaro Valley storehouses. Murra (1975, 1981) reconstructed this khipu's ethno-categories — it recorded goods by Andean classification, not Spanish.

**Who benefited:** The Wanka lord and his community. This is indigenous testimony *against* Spanish interests — documenting theft by the conquerors. The khipu distinguished "given" from "stolen," a distinction only the keeper could make.

**Strategic behavior:** The lord had incentive to maximize the "stolen" column. But he needed credibility in a Spanish court — inflating too far risks dismissal of the entire account. The optimal play: accurate enough to be believed, shaded toward the community. We cannot verify the numbers (the physical khipu is lost; we have only the Spanish scribe's transcription).

**Source:** Espinoza 1971 [1558–61]; Murra 1975; summarized in Urton & Chu 2015.

## Case 2: The 1542 Inquest — Inca History (Declaración de los Quipucamayos)

**Date:** 1542
**Location:** Cuzco
**Spanish questioner:** Cristóbal Vaca de Castro (governor of Peru, 1541–44)

**What was asked:** Vaca de Castro wanted to know Inca origins and dynastic history — specifically whether the Incas were native to the land or foreigners. He first asked Cuzco elders; they contradicted each other. The elders suggested finding the old khipukamayuqs.

**What was answered:** Four very old quipucamayoqs, found "wandering in the mountains, terrorized by the tyrants of the past," were brought to Cuzco with their khipu. They testified "in conformity" — all four agreed. They told the story of Atahualpa's captains (Chalcochima and Quizquiz) killing the khipukamayuqs and burning their khipu, attempting to erase history so "all was to begin anew" with Atahualpa.

**Who benefited:** Vaca de Castro got a coherent legitimizing narrative: the Incas were tyrants, Atahualpa was a usurper who destroyed records, Spanish rule was liberation. The keepers got survival and patronage. But note what the story *does*: it explains why no khipu contradict the Spanish account. The absence of evidence becomes evidence of Inca tyranny.

**Strategic behavior:** This is the most strategically shaped testimony in the corpus. The keepers told the new power exactly what it wanted to hear — that the old power was illegitimate and destructive. Whether Atahualpa actually ordered the mass killing of keepers is unverifiable; it is *convenient* for everyone at the table. Vaca de Castro gets legitimacy; the keepers get to live; the burned khipu can't contradict anyone.

**Source:** Collapina & Supno [1542/1608] 1921; discussed in Brokaw 2003, Urton 1990.

## Case 3: The 1567 Chucuito Visita — Census Cross-Check

**Date:** 1567
**Location:** Chucuito province, Lake Titicaca (Lupaqa kingdom)
**Spanish questioner:** Garci Diez de San Miguel (royal inspector)

**What was asked:** Diez asked whether the lower-moiety census khipu matched the upper-moiety census khipu — a direct audit.

**What was answered:** Martín Cusi (cacique principal, lower moiety) and Lope Martín Ninara (head khipu keeper, lower moiety) compared their khipu against Martín Cari's (cacique principal, upper moiety) khipu "section by section." They "conformed in all their parts/divisions and numbers of Indians in all the towns of both moieties" — *except* for the Canas Indians of Pomata: 20 in one khipu, 22 in the other.

**Who benefited:** The Spanish got a verified census (basis for tribute assessment). The keepers demonstrated professional competence. The Lupaqa kingdom — notably *not yet under encomienda* in 1567 — preserved a degree of autonomy by cooperating.

**Strategic behavior:** This is a mutual-monitoring game. Two keepers, each holding a copy of the census, cross-checked in front of the inspector. Neither could shade far without diverging from the other. The 20-vs-22 discrepancy (~10%) is the calibration point: it was noted but not punished. This suggests the tolerance band for "error" — or the equilibrium shading level both keepers independently chose.

The census itself recorded 20,080 households (~100,000 people): 15,778 Aymara, 4,129 Uru, 173 mitma. These are large numbers; a 10% shade across the board would be ~2,000 households of hidden tribute base.

**Source:** Diez de San Miguel [1567] 1964; Murra 1968 ("An Aymara Kingdom in 1567"); Urton 1998.

## Case 4: The 1579 Sacaca Case — Coca Tribute

**Date:** 1579
**Location:** Sacaca (Charcas region, modern Bolivia)
**Spanish questioner:** Spanish court (Audiencia)

**What was asked / case:** "El cacique principal e Yndios del Pueblo de Sacaca con los herederos de Dn Alonso de Montemayor, sobre demasia de Tributos" — the indigenous community sued the encomendero's heirs for *excess* tribute collection. A companion document: "Relación que hazen los quipocamayos de las chacaras de coca" — the khipu account of the coca fields.

**What was answered:** The quipocamayos produced the coca-field accounts from their khipu, documenting production and tribute paid.

**Who benefited:** The indigenous plaintiffs. This is khipukamayuq testimony *against* a Spanish encomendero family, in a Spanish court, using khipu as evidence of overpayment.

**Strategic behavior:** The keepers had maximum incentive to show high payments and low production — every number served the community's case for tribute reduction. But they testified in a formal legal proceeding where the encomendero's heirs could challenge. Credibility was essential. The equilibrium: accurate enough to survive scrutiny, shaded toward the plaintiffs.

**Source:** AGI Justicia 653, no. 2 (4 piezas) [1579]; cited in Quilter & Urton 2002.

## Case 5: The 1593 Canta Case — Official Misconduct

**Date:** 1593
**Location:** Canta province, central highlands
**Spanish questioner:** Don Juan Vargas Venegas (corregidor, justicia mayor, juez de residencia y cuentas)

**What was asked:** This was a *juicio de residencia* — a misconduct trial of outgoing officials. The quipucamayoc and shepherds of Canta gave account *against* Don Alonso de Armeta and his officials, covering liquidation of accounts, tribute rates paid by indigenous people, and community contributions from Huamantanga, Pacaraos, Hanan Piscas, Hurin Piscas, and the Atavillos.

**What was answered:** The khipukamayuq provided the tribute and contribution accounts used to assess whether Armeta had skimmed or overcharged.

**Who benefited:** Two parties: (1) the indigenous communities (if Armeta was overcharging, they get relief); (2) Vargas Venegas, the incoming corregidor, who gets ammunition against his predecessor. The keepers allied with the new Spanish power against the old.

**Strategic behavior:** Classic factional alignment. The keepers' optimal move is to side with the incoming official — he needs their testimony to convict, they need his protection. Numbers would be shaded to maximize Armeta's apparent guilt (higher "owed" vs. "paid" gaps). Vargas Venegas had no incentive to check their math carefully.

**Source:** Cajavilca Navarro (citing 1593 Canta records).

## Case 6: The 1602 Xauxa Road Account — Resistance Archiving

**Date:** 1602 (account covers ongoing period)
**Location:** Xauxa Valley, on the royal road
**Spanish questioner:** None — this was not testimony but discovered records. Diego Avalos y Figueroa reported it in *Miscelánea austral* (1602).

**What happened:** An old Indian was found on the road carrying a bundle of quipus he tried to hide. When challenged, he explained these were "the account he had to give to the Inca, when he [the Inca] returned from the other world, of all that had happened in the valley in his absence." The quipus recorded all Spaniards who had traveled the royal road — what they wanted, bought, and did, "both the good and the bad." The corregidor burned the accounts and punished the Indian.

**Who benefited:** Nobody in the short term. The keeper was maintaining records for a future Inca restoration — a 70-year resistance archive. The Spanish recognized the threat and destroyed it.

**Strategic behavior:** This is the long game. The keeper wasn't shading numbers for a Spanish auditor; he was preserving evidence for a regime change that never came. The fact that he recorded "both the good and the bad" suggests he was maintaining a genuine chronicle, not a polemic — its value depended on credibility with the future Inca, not the present Spanish.

**Source:** Avalos y Figueroa, *Miscelánea austral* (1602); cited in MacCormack 1985.

## The Structural Break: 1583

The Third Council of Lima (1581–83) classified khipu as idolatrous objects and ordered them burned (Tercer Concilio Limense 1583, acto 3, ch. 37). This is the hinge of the entire corpus.

**Before 1583:** Khipu are legitimate administrative tools. Keepers are valued experts. Testimony is professional (Cases 1–4).

**After 1583:** Keeping or reading khipu is criminal. Every keeper who testifies is confessing to idolatry. The incentive structure inverts: cooperation with Spanish authorities now carries the cost of self-incrimination.

Cases 5 (1593) and 6 (1602) fall after the break. In Case 5, the keeper testifies anyway — the protection of the incoming corregidor presumably outweighed the risk. In Case 6, the keeper hid his records — and was punished when discovered.

## Game-Theoretic Comparison

### Players and Payoffs

| Player | Wants | Fears |
|---|---|---|
| Khipukamayuq | Survival, status, community protection, professional monopoly | Punishment, exposure as liar, loss of monopoly, idolatry charge (post-1583) |
| Spanish questioner | Numbers for tribute; legitimacy narratives; ammunition vs. rivals | Being deceived; setting tribute too low (Crown angry) or too high (revolt) |
| Encomendero (when defendant) | Higher tribute intake; lower accountability | Court ruling for reduction; residencia conviction |
| Indigenous community | Lower tribute; land protection | Retaliation for testifying |
| Rival keeper (moiety counterpart) | Same as keeper | Being exposed by the other's divergent numbers |

### Equilibrium Strategies by Case

| Case | Keeper's Strategy | Observed Outcome | Primary Beneficiary |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Wanka 1533/58-61 | Document Spanish theft, credibly | Court accepted khipu evidence | Indigenous community |
| 2. Vaca de Castro 1542 | Tell the new power what it wants | Legitimacy narrative produced | Spanish (legitimacy) + keepers (survival) |
| 3. Chucuito 1567 | Mutual monitoring, ~10% tolerance | Census verified with minor discrepancy | Spanish (verified base) |
| 4. Sacaca 1579 | Testify against encomendero | Coca accounts entered as evidence | Indigenous community |
| 5. Canta 1593 | Ally with incoming official | Misconduct case against predecessor | Community + new corregidor |
| 6. Xauxa 1602 | Hide records for future Inca | Records burned, keeper punished | Nobody (failed long game) |

### Patterns

**1. The keeper almost always shades toward the indigenous community** — except in Case 2 (1542), where survival required serving the Spanish narrative. The 1542 case is the outlier that proves the rule: when the keeper's life depends on pleasing the questioner, the testimony flips.

**2. The beneficiary shifts with the questioner's faction.** In Cases 4 and 5, keepers testified against Spanish encomenderos/officials — but always *for* another Spanish authority (the court, the incoming corregidor). The keeper never testifies against the Spanish system as such; he picks the Spanish faction most aligned with community interests.

**3. Mutual monitoring constrains shading.** Case 3 (Chucuito) is the cleanest game: two keepers, each holding a copy, compared section by section. The 10% discrepancy band is the measurable output of this equilibrium. Neither keeper could shade beyond what the other would tolerate, because divergence exposes both.

**4. The 1583 break changed the game fundamentally.** Before: keepers are experts, testimony is professional, shading is moderate and calibrated. After: keepers are criminals, testimony is self-incrimination, the rational move is concealment (Case 6) or high-value alliance (Case 5, where the incoming corregidor's protection outweighed the idolatry risk).

**5. The information monopoly was the keeper's only asset — and it was wasting.** Each testimony gave the Spanish more understanding of the system. The keeper's optimal strategy was to be *just* useful enough to survive while revealing *as little as possible* about the encoding itself. Note what the keepers never testified to: *how* the khipu worked. They gave numbers, not methods. The Spanish got transcriptions but never the key. When the keepers died, the system died with them.

### The Meta-Game: Why We Can't Read Khipu

The game-theoretic analysis explains the central puzzle of khipu studies: why do we have 15–20 colonial transcriptions but no link between any transcription and a surviving physical khipu?

Because the keepers won the meta-game. They gave the Spanish *outputs* (numbers for tribute, narratives for legitimacy) while withholding the *encoding* (how color, knot, position, and structure combine). Each testimony was a strategic disclosure — enough to satisfy the questioner, never enough to make the keeper replaceable.

The Spanish thought they were extracting information. The keepers were managing a controlled leak. When the last keepers died or were killed, the leak sealed shut — leaving us with answers but no questions, transcriptions but no key.

## Limitations and Honest Caveats

1. **Primary sources not accessed.** This analysis relies on published secondary summaries. The AGI documents (Justicia 653, Lima 205, Charcas 37) and the Biblioteca Nacional manuscript (Legajo J #133, "Declaración de los Quipucamayos") need direct examination.

2. **The "shading" inference is structural, not proven.** I infer strategic behavior from incentive structures and the 20-vs-22 calibration point. I cannot prove any specific number was altered.

3. **Survivorship bias.** We only know about cases that produced surviving documents. Keepers who successfully concealed their records (like the Xauxa keeper tried to) leave no trace — except when caught.

4. **The 1542 case is overdetermined.** Four keepers testifying "in conformity" about Atahualpa's destruction of records is either genuine consensus or coordinated strategy. Game theory favors the latter, but the former is possible.

5. **Translation layers.** Every testimony passed through Quechua/Aymara → Spanish translation, then scribal transcription. Each layer is a strategic actor (the translator, the scribe). The game has more players than my model includes.

## What This Means for the Khipu Project

Charles's question — "aggregate their testimony as to what they testified to about and whose benefit it was" — yields a clear result: **khipukamayuq testimony systematically served indigenous community interests, except when survival required serving the Spanish questioner directly.**

For our structural work, the implication is:

- **Colonial transcriptions are not neutral readings.** They are strategic outputs of the games above. Any attempt to use them as ground truth for decipherment must discount for shading.
- **The 10% Chucuito band is a calibration tool.** Where we have colonial census numbers linked to khipu, assume ±10% strategic variance.
- **The keepers never revealed the encoding.** This means colonial sources will not give us the key — they give us only outputs. Our structural/file-format approach is the right path because it doesn't depend on testimony.
- **The "paper khipus" (transcriptions in colonial trials) are the most promising bridge** — but only if we treat them as what they are: negotiated numbers, not raw readings.
